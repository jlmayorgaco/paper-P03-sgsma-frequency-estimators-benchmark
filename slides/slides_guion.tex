{\Large\bfseries\color{ofbTeal} Field events define the stress model}\\[6pt]
{\small\itshape\color{ofbMuted}Say (verbatim):}\\[2pt]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.05em,itemsep=4pt,topsep=2pt]
\item Let me ground this in reality. Real inverter-driven disturbances look nothing like the clean sine wave we use in textbooks.
\item If you look at recorded events, Blue Cut, Odessa, the Kauai oscillation, you see phase jumps, fast rate-of-change of frequency, harmonics, and noise all arriving at the same time.
\item So instead of inventing convenient test signals, we let these real field events define the stress cases that our benchmark has to cover.
\item That keeps the evaluation honest, because the grid does not care about our assumptions.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{3pt}{\color{ofbBlue}$\rightarrow$ \itshape And here is the catch: the very instrument we use to watch these events also shapes what we conclude about them.}\\[2pt]
\vspace{4pt}
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=ofbTeal!8,colframe=ofbTeal,boxrule=0.7pt,arc=2pt,left=5pt,right=5pt,top=3pt,bottom=3pt]

{\Large\bfseries\color{ofbTeal} PMU streams show consequences}\\[6pt]
{\small\itshape\color{ofbMuted}Say (verbatim):}\\[2pt]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.05em,itemsep=4pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A standard PMU hands us a frequency value and a rate-of-change of frequency.
\item But during a fast inverter event, those numbers show the consequence, not always the cause.
\item And crucially, it is the estimator buried inside the PMU that decides what we actually see on the screen.
\item Two different estimators, looking at the very same event, can report two different stories.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{3pt}{\color{ofbBlue}$\rightarrow$ \itshape So before we can trust any analysis of an event, we first have to ask which estimator produced those numbers.}\\[2pt]
\vspace{4pt}

{\Large\bfseries\color{ofbTeal} Operating claim}\\[6pt]
{\small\itshape\color{ofbMuted}Say (verbatim):}\\[2pt]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.05em,itemsep=4pt,topsep=2pt]
\item The central claim of this work is that estimator selection has to be conditional.
\item It is not a single ranking. It depends on three things at once: the type of disturbance, the metric you actually care about, and your compute budget.
\item Change any one of those, and the best estimator can change with it.
\item So our job is not to crown a winner; it is to map out which method wins under which conditions.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{3pt}{\color{ofbBlue}$\rightarrow$ \itshape And to back that claim with evidence, we built three things.}\\[2pt]

{\Large\bfseries\color{ofbTeal} How error grows with stress: the scaling exponent}\\[6pt]
{\small\itshape\color{ofbMuted}Say (verbatim):}\\[2pt]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.05em,itemsep=4pt,topsep=2pt]
\item This is one of my favorite results, and it is more physics than benchmarking. If you take the log-log slope of error versus severity, you get a scaling exponent that classifies the estimators.
\item A slope of about one, in green, means error grows linearly with stress --- that is the Cramer-Rao-efficient regime, where the method is statistically as good as the noise allows. ESPRIT, MUSIC, TFT and UKF sit there.
\item A slope near zero, in red, means flat and bad --- saturated or broken, like TKEO and LKF. And a slope above one is super-linear collapse, which is where the textbook EKF lands under noise.
\item The exponent, not the single error number, predicts how a method behaves as an event gets worse.
\end{itemize}

{\Large\bfseries\color{ofbBlue} Why a sag breaks zero-crossing}\\[6pt]
{\small\itshape\color{ofbMuted}Say (verbatim):}\\[2pt]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.05em,itemsep=4pt,topsep=2pt]
\item The mechanism is simple physics. The timing error of a zero-crossing scales with the noise divided by the amplitude times the frequency.
\item A deep sag shrinks the amplitude, which flattens the slope of the waveform right at the crossing.
\item And once the slope is flat, even small noise creates false crossings. It is not a coding bug; it is the geometry of the signal.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{3pt}{\color{ofbBlue}$\rightarrow$ \itshape Harmonics tell a similar, family-by-family story.}\\[2pt]

{\small\itshape\color{ofbMuted}Say (verbatim):}\\[2pt]
